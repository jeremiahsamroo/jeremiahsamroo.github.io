Density related supply side regulatory constraints and housing affordability in American cities

Senior thesis, Princeton Economics · advised by Prof. Henrik Kleven · 2025–2026

“Land-use regulation makes housing expensive” is close to conventional wisdom, but the standard measure — the Wharton Land Use Regulatory Index (WRLURI) — bundles very different policies into one number. A density cap, a supply restriction, and a slow permitting office are different mechanisms with different remedies, and a single index can’t tell you which one binds.

My thesis decomposes the WRLURI into mechanism-specific sub-indices (density restrictions, supply restrictions, approval delays) and estimates each one’s effect on housing affordability across U.S. metropolitan areas, using 15M+ ACS/IPUMS microdata observations in a cross-sectional OLS design with state fixed effects. The headline result: approval delays are the binding, most robust margin. Density restrictions cut the other way on home values once urban/suburban composition is accounted for, while still worsening price-to-income affordability.

I treat the WRLURI throughout as what it is — a noisy proxy for true local regulatory stringency, built from a short survey with subjectively constructed factor weights — not as an instrument. That measurement error should attenuate the estimates toward zero, which makes the surviving approval-delay result more informative, not less. The thesis also documents a difference-in-differences attempt that the available index waves couldn’t support, and says why.
