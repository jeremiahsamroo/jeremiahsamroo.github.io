Density related supply side regulatory constraints and housing affordability in American cities

Senior thesis, Princeton Economics · advised by Prof. Henrik Kleven · 2025–2026

Orthodox economic wisdom would have it that land use regulation is a proximate contributor to inter-metropolitan heterogeneity in housing prices. There are several standard measures, however I chose to focus in on the Wharton Residential Land Use Regulatory Index (WRLURI), which bundles multiple different policy axes into one number. My central aim was to determine which of these axes functions as the binding constraint, thus making it the primary driver of housing price increases. We principally examined an index of density restrictions, an index of approval delay restrictions, and an index of explicit supply cap restrictions.

My thesis decomposes the WRLURI into mechanism-specific sub-indices (density restrictions, supply restrictions, approval delays) and estimates each one’s effect on housing affordability across U.S. metropolitan areas, using 15M+ ACS/IPUMS microdata observations in a cross-sectional OLS design with state fixed effects. Our principal result was that approval delays are the most robust, binding margin of any of the sub-indices of the WRLURI. Density restrictions cut the other way, observed to be negatively associated with price-to-income measures of housing affordability.

I treat the WRLURI throughout as what it is — a noisy proxy for true local regulatory stringency, built from a short survey with subjectively constructed factor weights — not as an instrument. That measurement error should attenuate the estimates toward zero, which makes the surviving approval-delay result more informative, not less. The thesis also documents a difference-in-differences attempt that the available index waves couldn’t support, and says why.
